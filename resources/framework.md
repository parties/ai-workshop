# The Four-Part Prompt Framework

A method for writing classroom prompts that make student thinking visible and assessable.

*Framework by Tim Swick and Mark Davis, presented at CSTA 2026.*

---

## The goal, stated plainly

This framework does **not** exist to get better output from an AI. It exists to make a
student's thinking **visible and assessable**.

That distinction governs everything below. A rewritten assignment that produces a nicer
essay but still lets a student type one sentence and submit the result is a failed rewrite.

The test for any rewrite: **what does the student have to know, decide, or judge before
they can even ask the question?** If the answer is still "nothing," it hasn't worked.

## The underlying problem

The problem was never AI. It's **underspecified human intent.**

Take a common assignment: *"Write a one-page explanation of the causes of the American
Revolution."*

A student needs to know nothing about the American Revolution to type that. There is no
thinking required before the request, so there is no thinking to observe. That is not a
cheating problem — it's a specification problem, and it existed before AI. AI just made it
visible.

The second risk is quieter and worse. When a whole class delegates the *judgment* — not the
writing, the judgment — to one model, the output isn't wrong. It's reasonable. It's the same
reasonable thing thirty times. That homogenization is the real cost.

---

## The four parts

Every prompt worth assigning specifies all four.

### 1. Verb — the thinking move

Name the **cognitive move**, not the product.

- **Good:** analyze, compare, critique, evaluate, generate options, simulate, prioritize, diagnose, refute
- **Weak:** write, make, create, do, summarize, explain

> **"Write" is not a thinking verb.** It names an artifact, not an operation. "Summarize" and
> "explain" are borderline — they can be legitimate cognitive moves, but they're also the two
> most common disguises for pure retrieval. Push for something with judgment in it.

### 2. Format — the shape that makes thinking visible

Constrain the output so a human can **evaluate** it.

- Examples: two-column table, decision tree, ranked list with justifications, annotated outline, dialogue between two positions, before/after comparison, matrix
- The format should make gaps in reasoning **structurally obvious**. A table with an empty cell shows a hole. A paragraph hides one.
- Prefer asking for a **step** in the work over a finished product. The finished product is where substitution hides.

### 3. Voice — the perspective being simulated

State whose point of view the response takes.

- Examples: a skeptic, a novice, a domain expert, a customer, a regulator, a competitor, a historian, a peer reviewer
- This does quiet pedagogical work: it teaches students that the model **simulates viewpoints**, it doesn't hold truth. Changing one word changes the entire answer, and noticing that is the lesson.
- A strong move: ask for the **same question from two conflicting voices**, then have the student account for the difference.

### 4. Context — the human constraints that matter

Audience, grade level, purpose, values, limits, setting.

**This is where human judgment lives, and it's the part everyone skips.** The model will
silently invent every one of these if you don't state them. The invented version is generic,
which is exactly why generic output comes back.

Context is also the part that carries the teacher's actual situation: what these students can
do, what they can't, how long they have, what equipment exists, what the district requires.

## Weak vs. rewritten

**Weak**

> Write an essay about AI in schools.

Invites vague coverage, opinion dumping, and total substitution. There is no student left in it.

**Rewritten**

> **Analyze the tradeoffs** *(verb)* of using AI tools in classrooms, **in a two-column table**
> *(format)*, **from the perspective of a school administrator** *(voice)*, **focusing on the
> decisions a middle school has to make about student use and academic integrity** *(context)*.

Same topic. Now the student has to know what a tradeoff is, hold an administrator's priorities
in mind, and judge which decisions actually matter. And the teacher can grade **the prompt**,
not just the output.

---

## Two standards worth holding

> **If students are skipping these steps, they're outsourcing their thinking.**

> **If students can't explain these four parts, they shouldn't be using AI yet.**

The second is a real, teachable, assessable standard — and a far better policy than "don't
use it," which has never worked anywhere.

---

## How to roll it out with students

1. Do one prompt in front of the class, thinking out loud.
2. Build one together as a group.
3. Let them go solo.
4. Put the four parts on a poster and leave it up.

Gradual release. Same as anything else you teach.
