Context
isn't abucket

The window doesn't fill up. It's torn down and rebuilt from scratch on every single turn — and an instruction can lose its grip on the model long before anything runs out of room.

Mechanism

Every turn is a cold start

A language model keeps nothing between messages. There is no session, no running memory, no buffer sitting on a server with your conversation in it.

When you press send, the client assembles one flat block of text — system prompt, then every previous message in the thread, then what you just typed — and runs it through the model in a single pass. The model reads all of it as if for the first time. Then it's thrown away.

The context window is the size limit on that re-sent block. Not a tank that fills. A page-length limit on a document that gets retyped, longer each time, before every reply.

Why this framing matters

If you teach the bucket metaphor, Projects look like magic. Once you teach reassembly, Projects are obvious: they're just text that gets pasted into the top of the block every time it's rebuilt. Same mechanism, no new machinery.

The part people get wrong

Overflow behaviour isn't the model's — it's the app's. On the raw API, too much text returns an error and nothing is silently dropped. Chat products each choose their own policy: cut the oldest turns, compress earlier ones into a summary, or fetch only relevant pieces on demand. "It forgets the beginning" describes one product's choice, not a law of LLMs.

Around turn 12 the plain lane is roughly 60% full and the rule is already at about half strength. Nothing has been deleted. That gap between the meters is the whole lesson: drift is a signal-to-noise problem, not a capacity problem. Running out of room is where it ends, not where it starts.

Diagnosis

Four reasons it slips

Only the last one involves running out of space. The first three are already at work in a window that's half empty.

Lost in the middle

Attention isn't spread evenly across the payload. Material at the very beginning and the very end gets used reliably; material buried in the middle gets used measurably less. This is a well-documented effect in long-context evaluation.

A rule given on turn 2 starts out at the end of a short payload — the strongest position there is. Twenty turns later it hasn't moved, but the payload has grown around it, and it now sits dead centre. Same text, worse seat.

Dilution

Present is not the same as weighted. Fifty words of instruction compete against forty turns of code, questions, and corrections — all of it equally readable, all of it pulling.

The instruction never got weaker. Everything else got louder.

Self-conditioning

The model's own previous replies are part of its input. Whatever it did on turn 5 is evidence for what turn 20 should look like.

So one small slip becomes precedent, and precedent compounds. This is why drift accelerates instead of holding steady — and why correcting early costs far less than correcting late.

Eviction and compaction

Only now does capacity matter. The app hits its ceiling and does something: drop the oldest turns, or squeeze earlier ones into a summary.

Compaction is the sneakier one. The gist survives, the specifics evaporate, and the transcript still looks complete — so the loss is invisible right up until the model contradicts something you're certain you said.

The other caveat worth naming out loud

Short pinned instructions are included verbatim. Large uploaded knowledge bases often aren't — many systems retrieve the relevant chunks instead of inserting the whole library. So "I put it in the Project" guarantees availability, not presence. If a specific sentence must be obeyed every time, it belongs in the instructions, not in an attached document.

Talk track

Running this with a room

Open by surfacing the wrong model — don't correct it yet

Ask the room to describe, in their own words, what happens to a long chat. You will reliably get some version of "it fills up and forgets the oldest stuff." Write it on the board. Leave it there.

"Hold that. We're going to come back and change one word in it."

It's a productive wrong answer — right about the ceiling, wrong about the mechanism. Letting people commit to it out loud makes the correction land harder than pre-empting it.

Land the reassembly idea before touching the simulator

Physical demo that costs nothing: read a sentence aloud from an index card, then hand the card to someone and walk out of the room. Come back with no memory of it. To continue, someone has to hand you every card again, in order, including your own previous ones.

"That's every turn. Not a conversation — a stack of cards re-dealt, one card longer, and I read the whole stack again before I say anything."

  • The model isn't remembering. The app is re-sending.
  • The window is a limit on the stack height, not a container being filled.
  • My own previous cards are in the stack — that detail pays off in the third panel.
Drive the simulator — step, don't play

Play is for a second pass. First time through, step manually and stop at three moments:

  • Turn 2. The rule is planted. Both lanes read 100. Point out the amber block sitting at the bottom of the plain lane's stack — right now it's the most recent thing said, the best possible position.
  • Turn 12. Occupancy near 60%, plain fidelity near 50%. This is the moment worth the pause. Nothing has been deleted, no limit has been hit, and the rule is already at half strength. Ask what they think happened.
  • Turn 19–20. Eviction. The amber block falls off the plain lane entirely. The project lane also overflows — but it sheds conversation, not the rule.

"Both of them ran out of room. Only one of them lost the instruction. That's the whole argument for Projects."

Corrections to make explicitly, once they've seen it

Go back to the board and fix the sentence from the opening. Three edits:

  • "Fills up" → "gets rebuilt." Nothing accumulates; the payload is reassembled every turn.
  • "Forgets" → "the app drops or compresses." It's a product decision, and different products decide differently. The raw API just errors.
  • "When it's full" → "long before it's full." Position, dilution, and self-conditioning are already degrading things at half capacity.
Close on practice, not theory

The four habits that fall out of this, in order of payoff:

  • Start a new chat more often than feels necessary. The strongest thing Projects do isn't persistence — it's letting every new thread start from a clean floor instead of inheriting forty turns of accumulated drift.
  • Correct on the first slip. Self-conditioning means a tolerated mistake becomes the pattern. Cheap on turn 6, expensive on turn 26.
  • Restate the constraint right before the work that depends on it. The end of the payload is a high-attention position. Using it is free.
  • Pin the short rules, not the whole library. Pinned content is spent budget, and big attachments may be retrieved rather than included.

"You're not managing its memory. You're managing what gets retyped, and where in the page it lands."

About the model behind the demo. The window is scaled to 1,000 units so the arithmetic stays visible on a projector — real windows run to hundreds of thousands of tokens. The fidelity meter is a teaching model, not a measurement: it combines position in the payload, the instruction's shrinking share of the whole, and compounding self-conditioning. The shapes reflect documented long-context behaviour; the specific numbers are illustrative. Worth saying out loud to a room of teachers.
